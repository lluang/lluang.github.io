### 1. Panel Introductions and Pathways into Analytics Project and Product Management 

*What brought each of you into managing analytics and data science initiatives? For those studying various areas of analytics considering this path, what foundational habits or mindsets proved most essential when transitioning from hands-on modeling to product or project leadership?*

Prasad traced a twenty-year healthcare career that began in software engineering and progressed through business system analysis, product ownership, project management, and product management. Working on unstructured healthcare data such as reading medical charts pulled him into NLP, analytics, AI, and now GenAI. Although he is not a data scientist, his strength lies in identifying and defining the business problem and aligning solutions to it. His core advice to analysts was to invest deeply in understanding the domain and where value is created--specifically asking whether a problem genuinely requires AI or GenAI, simpler analytics, or straightforward software engineering.

Jeremy described a similar technical origin: a decade as a prototypical engineer happy to wear headphones and write code all day. As he moved into team lead roles, he was reluctantly pulled onto customer troubleshooting calls and discovered he loved talking with users--particularly catching passing, offhand remarks that revealed unaddressed problems the company could solve. That realization led him into ten years of startup product management (typically entering around product-market fit from seed through Series C) and ultimately to Local Logic, where his team packages location-insight data at scale across more than 100,000 neighborhoods for platforms like Zillow and Homes.com. His advice echoed Prasad's: data is a powerful unlock, but there are infinite possible solutions, and an impressive technical solution still fails if it does not solve a validated problem for the specific people expected to adopt it.

Rob took a markedly non-linear route. After studying software engineering, he pivoted in his third year at Robert Morris to psychology, entered healthcare as a pharmacy technician, and moved into analytics and formulary decision-making at UPMC to understand how decisions were made. That curiosity led into change management and eventually AI consulting at Highmark Health. He credited behavioral psychology with helping him probe past stakeholder requests for "shiny new toys" to uncover the root cause of a problem. His foundational advice for both project and product roles was twofold: stay curious about the underlying problem, and cultivate a service mindset that places the user and the product ahead of personal ego.

Antonio began in mechatronics engineering and co-founded a startup monitoring building energy usage and recommending efficiency improvements, which forced him to pair technical execution with close customer contact. He later worked as a Technical Program Manager at companies including Lyft and Zillow before founding a management-sciences venture and pursuing an Engineering Management degree at CMU. Complementing the other panelists' emphasis on customer value, he highlighted a recurring internal failure mode he sees as a project manager: teams become so absorbed in coding or talking to customers that they forget to talk to one another. Leveraging the collective experience across internal, external, and customer teams is essential to delivering value.

### 2. Defining Core Responsibilities: What Is the Role of the Project and Product Manager on a Team? 

*What is the primary role of the project manager and product manager on an analytics team, how do their day-to-day responsibilities differ from one another in practice, and how do they collaborate with technical modelers and stakeholders?*

- Product Management (vision, value, and problem space): Product management focuses on customer value, long-term vision, and behavioral data--understanding not merely what users say their problem is, but what they actually experience in their workflows. In enterprise settings, this involves decomposing a strategic initiative (such as healthcare payment integrity) into coordinated capabilities--workflow management, intelligent targeting analytics, and AI-assisted auditing--while balancing both functional outcomes and financial return. Product managers typically establish multi-year visions (accounting for future scalability), one-year roadmaps, and quarterly milestones.
- Product Ownership and Business Analysis (tactical, inward-facing detail): Originating from Scrum as an accountability rather than strictly a job title, the Product Owner role focuses inward on delivery--refining backlogs, clarifying requirements for sprint teams, and working closely with operational end users to specify detailed workflows. In large enterprises, dedicated Product Owners and Business Analysts absorb this tactical layer so Product Managers can focus externally on strategy, leadership, and market growth; in smaller organizations, a Product Manager (or even an executive such as a CMO or CFO) carries the accountability directly.
- Project Management (finite execution and objective oversight): Whereas product ownership is indefinite ("defining what the finish line looks like"), project management operates on a finite timeline with a defined start and end ("quarterbacking the effort across the finish line"). Project managers coordinate resources, track progress against value-tied milestones, and flag dependencies and risks early with mitigation plans. A distinct source of value is their "third-party perspective": being part of the team without directly building the solution gives them the objectivity to spot drift, deliver candid feedback, and challenge whether an impressive feature is actually delivering its intended ROI.
- Program Management (cross-team strategic alignment): Sitting adjacent to project management at a higher altitude, program management coordinates multi-team initiatives spanning one to two years--integrating larger strategic goals into individual team backlogs without derailing day-to-day execution, and acting as the "voice of the team outside" with functions such as legal, marketing, and sales.
- Scale and hat-wearing: In a 50,000-person enterprise, each of these accountabilities may have a dedicated specialist and executive sponsor. In a startup or small analytics team, a single individual--often the analyst--must recognize and wear multiple hats simultaneously.

### 3. Navigating Asymmetric Expertise Across Technical Modelers and Customer Domain Experts 

*Product and project managers in analytics frequently operate at the intersection of two distinct groups whose specialized knowledge exceeds their own: advanced quantitative analysts (such as operations researchers, data scientists, and AI scientists) on the technical side, and customer or operational subject matter experts on the business side. How do managers establish credibility, evaluate trade-offs, and steer alignment when they are neither the deepest mathematical authority nor the foremost operational expert in the room?*

- Leading through inquiry and connection: Openly acknowledging the limits of one's own technical depth builds trust. Credibility is earned by understanding the goal, scope, and level of effort, asking targeted questions that prompt experts to reflect and stay focused, and acting as a connector who brings missing technical and business experts into the same room to decide together.
- Co-creation over prescription: Friction between product and engineering typically arises when technical teams feel handed pre-baked solutions. Effective managers keep business stakeholders focused on articulating the problem, then convene architects, analysts, and engineering leads with the problem statement and existing system context to co-design the approach. Architecture and technology choices belong to technical experts, while product ensures those choices remain anchored to business value.
- Transparent multi-criteria scoring for trade-offs: When multiple reasonable approaches exist and not every stakeholder can be satisfied, structured decision-making reduces resentment. Running parallel business and technical evaluation sessions--mapping pros and cons across business value, build cost, technology longevity (including rapid AI model turnover), maintenance, future enhancements, and technical debt, and scoring options on a shared 1–5 scale--creates shared visibility into where maximum value lies. Even stakeholders who do not get their preferred option accept the outcome when they clearly understand the rationale.
- Pre-alignment ("the meeting before the meeting") and escalation: Controversial proposals regarding scope, approach, or pricing should never be introduced for the first time in a full group meeting. Holding 1-on-1 pre-meetings with the most opinionated leaders lets stakeholders vent privately, surfaces valid flaws without forcing defensiveness, prevents group pile-ons, and overcomes hierarchy-induced silence when subordinates hesitate to contradict their boss in public. When genuine impasses remain, an executive sponsor must serve as the tiebreaker. Underpinning all of these tactics is a "people first" orientation.

### 4. Disentangling Tool Requests from Decision Problems

*How do project and product managers evaluate an incoming stakeholder request that specifies a predetermined solution—such as building a generative AI assistant, an optimization tool, or a dashboard—to uncover the root operational decision that actually requires analytical support?*

- Reframing from solution back to problem: Rather than accepting a tool request at face value, managers gently pause and invite the stakeholder to teach them the underlying workflow bottleneck so it can be communicated accurately to the technical team. Problem discovery often reveals that a requested build is unnecessary--for instance, discovering that an existing product already solves the problem, but poor design kept the user from finding it.
- Balancing individual requests against systemic fit: In multi-customer or enterprise-wide products, a bespoke fix tailored to one vocal requester can poorly fit or alienate dozens of other users facing a related problem. Product leadership must design for the shared underlying need, making conscious exceptions only for outsized strategic accounts.
- Opportunity cost and constructive pushback: A stakeholder request is not automatically a priority. Maintaining clear visibility into the full roadmap allows delivery leads to surface the opportunity cost of chasing a "shiny object"--making explicit what existing commitments will be delayed--and to bring technical SMEs directly into the room to explain feasibility and true cost. Anchoring these debates to the shared problem and goal rather than interpersonal dynamics ensures that the voice with the sharpest insight into the immediate problem guides the decision.

### 5. Facilitating Direct Stakeholder Access vs. Acting as a Filter

*What is the project or product manager's strategy for enabling direct, ongoing communication between technical modelers and end users, and how do they prevent the management layer from becoming a game of telephone that obscures domain nuance?*

- Direct inclusion with structured discipline: Bringing engineering leads and adjacent product representatives directly into business meetings ensures everyone hears domain nuance "from the horse's mouth." To keep these sessions productive, internal teams align beforehand on who leads the conversation, what outcomes are needed, and strict ground rules--most notably separating discovery from solutioning so that requirements-gathering meetings remain focused on understanding problems rather than pitching premature fixes. Post-meeting internal debriefs reconcile any divergent interpretations.
- Protecting technical time and avoiding passive attendance: Direct access must be purposeful rather than indiscriminate. Pulling engineers onto early or exploratory calls "just to listen" with cameras off trains technical staff to view customer meetings as wasted time. Managers earn the right to include engineers by being selective, building technical questions explicitly into the agenda, and ensuring technical expertise is actively solicited and valued.
- Requirements documents as entry gates: Business Requirements Documents (BRDs) and Product Requirements Documents (PRDs) serve a dual purpose beyond instructing developers. They act as a litmus test for whether the business and product sides can clearly articulate what they want before consuming engineering capacity; if a need cannot yet be articulated on paper, the right step is coaching the stakeholder rather than scheduling an engineering build.

### Open Audience Q&A: Prototyping, Scope Control, and Analytics Management in the GenAI Era 

The open Q&A extended the panel's core themes into rapid experimentation, scope governance, and the structural changes introduced by generative AI and agentic systems.

- Scoping versus rapid experimentation: To avoid endless strategy paralysis, teams treat blind spots as triggers to "think in experiments." When stakeholders are stuck in abstract vision statements or a key user behavior is unknown, rapid prototyping--accelerated dramatically by AI coding tools--puts a tangible artifact ("the art of the possible") in front of users within days rather than committing an engineering squad for multiple sprints. Discovering a false assumption cheaply is treated as a major win that feeds directly back into sharper requirements.
- Containing scope creep ("putting wings on a car") and true agility: Once an initial build is delivered, stakeholders routinely request major additions before launch. Effective containment combines three mechanisms: quantifying Cost of Delay (putting a concrete monthly revenue loss and competitive risk on holding a release to negotiate shipping core value first and iterating second), maintaining visibility of the original BRD/PRD goal to force an explicit trade-off between mid-build architectural redesign and incremental delivery, and using lightweight, executive-signed scope charters (such as a single slide defining in-scope items, out-of-scope items, and goals) alongside decisioning authority in the room. True agility--defined by early, frequent end-user check-ins rather than fixed-scope Waterfall chopped into sprints--becomes even more critical with generative AI, where non-deterministic outputs require separating controlled prototypes from production reality, designing human-in-the-loop accountability, and intentionally over-communicating expectations.
- Evals, drift, and behavioral alignment for non-deterministic systems: Evaluations and benchmarks are the least glamorous yet most decisive prerequisite for taking AI into production. Teams rarely possess true gold-standard datasets, and production data variability quickly invalidates proof-of-concept benchmarks. Sustainable AI deployment must therefore be framed as ongoing business process transformation--incorporating golden datasets, bias safeguards for marginalized groups, human-in-the-loop oversight, and recurring quarterly governance reviews to verify consistent behavior across model version updates. Because LLMs read specifications literally and exploit ambiguity (taking shortcuts like a child told to "clean your room" or reward-hacking by fabricating references to satisfy a citation rule), requirements must become far more explicit. Unlike human engineers who share tacit organizational knowledge, coding and workflow agents behave like "very confident interns" that require explicit out-of-scope boundaries--codified directly into reusable instruction skills--to prevent unintended code drift. Constraining agents is less like painting deterministic rules from scratch and more like sculpting marble: goal alignment is relatively straightforward, whereas behavioral alignment is exceptionally difficult.
- Shifting roles, Responsible AI, and preserving boundaries of ownership: Generative AI creates a paradox: building has never been easier, yet practitioners must understand a wider swath of adjacent domains--such as cloud infrastructure and information security--to design effective guardrails. Product management is shifting left from writing static documents toward acting as technical "product builders" who deploy throwaway "vision type" prototypes for stakeholder feedback, use custom LLM agents with tailored instruction files to draft artifacts and detect missing requirements gaps, and focus written PRDs on audience, distribution, positioning, and pricing. Simultaneously, software engineers are shifting toward architecture, formal technical specifications, and supervising coding agents. Across all of these shifts, AI remains a force multiplier subject to "garbage in, garbage out," and the binding constraint is increasingly policy, contractual, and ethical governance ("should we?") rather than technical feasibility ("can we?"). Navigating this safely requires intellectual honesty about the boundaries of one's own expertise, structured internal guardrail frameworks, and preserving a disciplined handoff from Requirements to Technical Design to Product--ensuring that detailed cloud, security, regulatory, and data-governance execution remains owned by technical and InfoSec experts rather than collapsed into product ownership.
